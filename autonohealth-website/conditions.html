BP
Heart & circulation

High blood pressure

Blood pressure that remains above the recommended range over time. It often causes no symptoms, so accurate repeat measurements matter.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Blood pressure reflects the force of blood against artery walls. A diagnosis is based on properly taken, repeated readings—not one stressful or unusual measurement. Long-term elevation raises the risk of heart, brain, kidney, and eye complications.

What people may notice

Most people notice nothing. Headache, dizziness, or feeling “off” are not reliable ways to know your blood pressure; measurement is the only way to identify it accurately.

When to get urgent help

A reading higher than 180/120 mm Hg with chest pain, shortness of breath, weakness, vision change, or difficulty speaking is an emergency—call emergency services. Without those symptoms, repeat the reading and contact a healthcare professional promptly.

Useful tracking

Record readings with date, time, arm, position, and relevant notes. A consistent home technique and a trend over time are more useful than isolated numbers.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
DASH-style eating patternStrong evidenceConsistent evidence supports blood pressure reduction; sodium reduction can add benefit.
Structured physical activityStrong evidenceRegular movement is a core lifestyle component of blood pressure management.
Guideline-supported medicationsStrong evidenceMedication selection depends on readings, cardiovascular risk, other conditions, and patient factors.
Magnesium supplementsLimited / mixedTrials suggest only a small average reduction; supplements are not equivalent to established therapy.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: 2025 AHA/ACC guidance, NHLBI DASH overview, and NIH magnesium fact sheet.
LDL
Heart & circulation · Metabolic health

Hyperlipidemia

Elevated levels of one or more blood lipids or lipoproteins, such as LDL cholesterol or triglycerides. Hyperlipidemia is a common form of dyslipidemia and usually causes no symptoms.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Hyperlipidemia describes elevated cholesterol, triglyceride, or lipoprotein levels measured with blood testing. Higher exposure to plaque-forming particles—especially LDL-containing particles—over time can promote atherosclerosis and increase heart attack and stroke risk. Evaluation also considers overall cardiovascular risk, family history, health conditions, and possible secondary causes.

What people may notice

Most people notice nothing, so a lipid panel is needed to identify the condition. Very high inherited cholesterol can sometimes cause fatty skin deposits or a pale ring around the cornea, but the absence of visible signs does not rule it out.

When to get urgent help

Hyperlipidemia itself is usually not an emergency. Call emergency services for chest pressure, sudden weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, or other heart attack or stroke warning signs. Severe, persistent upper-abdominal pain with vomiting also needs urgent assessment because very high triglycerides can contribute to pancreatitis.

Useful tracking

Keep lipid-panel results, medication names and doses, adherence, side effects, blood pressure, smoking status, diabetes or kidney health, activity, and eating-pattern changes together. Family history of early cardiovascular disease or very high cholesterol can materially change risk assessment.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Heart-healthy lifestyle patternStrong evidenceNutrition, regular activity, avoiding tobacco, healthy sleep, and weight management when relevant form the foundation; the plan should address the specific lipid pattern and overall risk.
Statin therapy for appropriate risk groupsStrong evidenceStatins lower LDL cholesterol and reduce heart attack and stroke risk. Whether to start one—and at what intensity—depends on cardiovascular risk, lipid levels, conditions, and shared decision-making.
Guideline-directed nonstatin therapyStrong evidenceEzetimibe, bempedoic acid, PCSK9-targeting medicines, or other therapies may be appropriate when statins are not enough, are not tolerated, or a person’s risk and lipid disorder call for a different strategy.
Supplements marketed for cholesterolLimited / mixedEffects and product quality vary. Red yeast rice may contain a statin-like compound with similar interactions and harms, while other products often have smaller or uncertain effects; supplements should not replace prescribed therapy.
A lipid result is interpreted in context rather than as a stand-alone pass/fail number. Treatment goals and the need for medication depend on the lipid abnormality, age, cardiovascular history and risk, other health conditions, pregnancy, tolerability, and preferences.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline summary, NHLBI blood cholesterol overview, NHLBI treatment overview, and NCCIH cholesterol supplement review.
T2
Metabolic health

Type 2 diabetes

A chronic condition in which the body does not use insulin effectively and, over time, may not make enough to keep blood glucose in a healthy range.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Type 2 diabetes is diagnosed with validated blood tests. Management is individualized and may include diabetes education, nutrition and activity support, weight management when relevant, monitoring, medication, and attention to heart, kidney, eye, nerve, and foot health.

What people may notice

Some people have increased thirst or urination, fatigue, blurry vision, recurrent infections, or slow-healing wounds; many have few or no early symptoms. Symptoms alone cannot confirm diabetes.

When to get urgent help

Confusion, severe weakness, persistent vomiting, trouble breathing, or signs of major dehydration require urgent assessment. Anyone using glucose-lowering medicines should know their personal low-glucose plan.

Useful tracking

Depending on the care plan: glucose readings, medication use, meals, movement, sleep, symptoms, and appointments. Trends are most useful when interpreted with the individual’s goals and treatment.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Individualized nutrition supportStrong evidenceHealthy eating patterns and diabetes self-management education are foundational, with no single plan for everyone.
Regular physical activityStrong evidenceActivity can improve glucose management and cardiovascular health; plans should fit ability and treatment.
Individualized medication therapyStrong evidenceEvidence-based medication choice now accounts for glucose goals, heart and kidney health, weight, cost, and preferences.
Supplements marketed for blood sugarLimited / mixedEvidence varies by product, preparation, population, and outcome; quality and interactions also matter.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026, CDC healthy eating, and CDC physical activity guidance.
Zz
Sleep

Chronic insomnia

Ongoing difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or getting restorative sleep despite adequate opportunity—together with meaningful daytime effects.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Insomnia is more than an occasional poor night. Evaluation considers timing, duration, daytime impairment, sleep schedule, substances, medications, mental health, pain, breathing-related sleep disorders, and other contributing conditions.

What people may notice

Trouble initiating or maintaining sleep, waking earlier than intended, fatigue, irritability, concentration difficulty, or worry about sleep. A sleep diary can help reveal patterns.

When to get urgent help

Do not drive if dangerously sleepy. Seek prompt help for severe mood symptoms, suicidal thoughts, breathing pauses during sleep, or sudden episodes of losing muscle control or falling asleep unexpectedly.

Useful tracking

Bedtime, estimated time to fall asleep, awakenings, wake time, naps, caffeine or alcohol timing, and next-day function. Focus on patterns rather than scoring a single night as good or bad.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomniaStrong evidenceCBT-I is recommended as first-line treatment for adults with chronic insomnia.
Consistent sleep routineModerate evidenceHelpful as part of a broader plan, but “sleep hygiene” alone may not resolve chronic insomnia.
Short-term prescription optionsModerate evidenceBenefits and harms vary by medicine and person; shared decision-making and reassessment are important.
Melatonin for chronic insomniaLimited / mixedSome outcomes may improve modestly, but findings are mixed and differ from uses such as jet lag.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: American College of Physicians insomnia guideline and NCCIH sleep and complementary approaches review.
AL
Allergy & immune

Seasonal allergies

An immune response to airborne allergens such as tree, grass, or weed pollen that can cause sneezing, congestion, a runny nose, and itchy or watery eyes.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Seasonal allergies, also called seasonal allergic rhinitis or hay fever, happen when the immune system reacts to airborne allergens that are more common during certain times of year. Common triggers include pollen from trees, grasses, and weeds, as well as outdoor mold spores.

What people may notice

Sneezing, a clear runny nose, nasal congestion, and itching of the nose, eyes, or throat are common. Watery or red eyes may occur too. Symptoms often return during the same season or after outdoor exposure and usually occur without a true fever.

When to get urgent help

Seek medical advice when symptoms are persistent, disrupt sleep or daily activities, are difficult to distinguish from another condition, or are accompanied by wheezing or poorly controlled asthma. Trouble breathing, facial or throat swelling, or other signs of a severe allergic reaction require urgent care.

Useful tracking

Record symptom timing, outdoor exposure, local pollen levels, medicines used, and response. Tree pollen is often more prominent in spring, grass pollen in late spring and summer, and weed pollen—including ragweed—in late summer and fall, although timing varies by climate, weather, and location.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Intranasal corticosteroidsStrong evidenceOften the most effective medication class for persistent or moderate-to-severe nasal symptoms, especially congestion. Benefit is best with correct technique and regular use during the symptomatic season.
Second-generation oral antihistaminesStrong evidenceCan reduce sneezing, itching, and a runny nose. They are generally less effective for congestion than intranasal corticosteroids, and newer agents are usually preferred over sedating first-generation antihistamines.
Intranasal antihistaminesStrong evidenceCan act quickly and improve several nasal symptoms. They may be used alone or, for some people with more persistent symptoms, with an intranasal corticosteroid.
Allergen immunotherapyStrong evidenceAllergy shots or certain FDA-approved under-the-tongue tablets may be considered when symptoms remain troublesome, avoidance and medicines are insufficient or undesirable, and a relevant allergen has been confirmed.
Reduce pollen exposureModerate evidenceChecking local pollen levels, keeping windows closed during high-pollen periods, changing clothes after outdoor activity, and showering before bed may reduce exposure and symptoms. Complete avoidance is usually unrealistic.
Saline nasal rinsingModerate evidenceSaline irrigation may improve nasal symptoms and can be used alone or alongside medication. Use distilled, sterile, or previously boiled and cooled water, and clean the device as directed.
Oral or topical decongestantsModerate evidenceMay provide short-term congestion relief but have important limitations. Oral decongestants can cause insomnia, palpitations, or increased blood pressure and are not appropriate for everyone. Decongestant nasal sprays can cause rebound congestion when used longer than directed.
Butterbur and other supplementsLimited / mixedEvidence is inconsistent, product quality varies, and some butterbur products may contain harmful pyrrolizidine alkaloids. Supplements should not be assumed to be safer than standard treatments.
Medication choice depends on the main symptoms, symptom severity, other health conditions, age, pregnancy status, and potential interactions. A pharmacist or clinician can help select an option and demonstrate correct nasal-spray technique.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: AAAAI rhinitis overview, ACAAI hay fever overview, Rhinitis 2020 practice parameter update, 2024 JAMA allergic rhinitis review.
Mi
Brain & nerves

Migraine

A neurological condition with recurring attacks that may include moderate-to-severe head pain, nausea, sensory sensitivity, and—sometimes—aura.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Migraine is more than a bad headache. It is a recurring neurological disorder whose attacks can unfold in phases and last from hours to days. Diagnosis is based mainly on the symptom pattern and examination; aura can cause temporary visual, sensory, or speech changes in some people.

What people may notice

Head pain may be throbbing, one-sided, and worsened by routine activity, but presentations vary. Nausea, vomiting, fatigue, and sensitivity to light, sound, or smells are common. Some people have warning symptoms before pain or lingering fatigue and concentration difficulty afterward.

When to get urgent help

Call emergency services for a sudden explosive or “worst-ever” headache, a new headache after head injury, or headache with new weakness, confusion, loss of consciousness, seizure, fever with a stiff neck, or unfamiliar speech or vision changes. A meaningful change in an established headache pattern also needs prompt medical review.

Useful tracking

Record attack date and duration, pain and associated symptoms, disability, possible triggers, menstrual timing when relevant, acute medicines and response, and headache-free days. A diary helps distinguish patterns and reveals frequent acute-medicine use that may worsen headaches.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Individualized acute medicationStrong evidenceNonopioid pain relievers can help some mild-to-moderate attacks, while migraine-specific medicines such as triptans or gepants may be used for more disabling attacks or when simpler options do not work.
Preventive medication when indicatedStrong evidenceSeveral established and newer options can reduce attack frequency or burden. Choice depends on attack pattern, other conditions, pregnancy potential, side effects, access, and preferences.
Regular routines and behavioral strategiesModerate evidenceConsistent sleep and meals, hydration, exercise as tolerated, relaxation, cognitive behavioral strategies, or biofeedback can support a broader plan; trigger patterns are individual and overly restrictive avoidance can be counterproductive.
Magnesium, riboflavin, and other supplementsLimited / mixedEvidence and safety vary by product and dose. Migraine-study doses can exceed routine supplement limits, so professional review is important.
A new or changed headache should not be self-labeled as migraine. Treatment selection also depends on cardiovascular history, pregnancy, other medicines, attack frequency, nausea or vomiting, and prior response.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: NINDS migraine overview, AHRQ acute migraine evidence review, American Headache Society guidance library, and NCCIH complementary approaches review.
GI
Digestive health

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)

A chronic condition in which reflux of stomach contents causes repeated troublesome symptoms or complications over time.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Occasional reflux is common. GERD is the more persistent condition in which stomach contents repeatedly move into the esophagus and cause bothersome symptoms or complications. A clinician can often begin evaluation from the history, but testing may be needed for alarm symptoms, an unclear diagnosis, or symptoms that do not improve.

What people may notice

Typical symptoms are heartburn and regurgitation, sometimes worse after meals or while lying down. Chest pain, nausea, swallowing difficulty, cough, or hoarseness can occur, but these symptoms have other possible causes and do not prove reflux.

When to get urgent help

Chest pain can be cardiac—call emergency services for new or severe chest pressure, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, faintness, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw. Prompt medical assessment is also needed for trouble or pain with swallowing, persistent vomiting, vomiting blood, black stools, loss of appetite, or unexplained weight loss.

Useful tracking

Record symptom timing, meals and personal triggers, lying down, sleep disruption, medicines and response, swallowing problems, and any bleeding or weight change. Note whether over-the-counter treatment is needed repeatedly rather than simply escalating it.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Proton pump inhibitorsStrong evidencePPIs are the most effective medicines for typical GERD symptoms and healing reflux-related esophageal injury. Timing, duration, and the lowest effective ongoing dose should be individualized.
Weight management when relevantModerate evidenceWeight loss can improve symptoms for people with overweight or obesity, but advice should be realistic and individualized.
Meal timing and nighttime positioningModerate evidenceAvoiding meals within about three hours of lying down and elevating the upper body can help nighttime symptoms. Broad food bans are less useful than identifying personal triggers.
H2-receptor blockers and antacidsModerate evidenceThese can help selected mild or intermittent symptoms, but antacids do not heal esophageal inflammation and repeated use should prompt review.
Persistent cough, hoarseness, or asthma symptoms have many causes. Improvement during acid suppression alone does not confirm that reflux caused an extraesophageal symptom.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: NIDDK definition and complications, NIDDK symptoms and warning signs, AGA personalized GERD guidance, and ACG acid reflux overview.
Lu
Respiratory health

Asthma

A chronic lung condition with variable airway inflammation and narrowing that can cause recurring wheeze, cough, chest tightness, and shortness of breath.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Asthma causes symptoms and airflow limitation that vary over time. Viral infections, allergens, smoke, air pollution, exercise, cold air, and other exposures may trigger symptoms. Diagnosis should be confirmed by a healthcare professional, usually with the symptom pattern and lung-function testing when feasible.

What people may notice

Wheezing, cough—often at night or early morning—shortness of breath, and chest tightness are typical. Symptoms may come and go, worsen with specific triggers, wake someone from sleep, limit usual activity, or require reliever medicine more often.

When to get urgent help

Follow the written asthma action plan during worsening symptoms. Call emergency services if breathing remains very hard or prescribed reliever medicine is not helping, or for blue or gray lips, severe drowsiness, confusion, inability to speak comfortably, or rapid deterioration.

Useful tracking

Record daytime and nighttime symptoms, activity limits, reliever use, missed work or school, triggers, inhaler adherence and technique, and flare-ups needing urgent care or oral steroids. Track peak flow only if it is part of the individual action plan.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Inhaled corticosteroid–containing therapyStrong evidenceICS-containing treatment addresses airway inflammation and reduces severe flare-ups and asthma deaths. The appropriate inhaler and schedule depend on age, severity, local approvals, and the person’s plan.
Correct inhaler technique and adherenceStrong evidenceTechnique and regular use should be checked before stepping treatment up; the device must fit the person’s ability and access.
Written asthma action planStrong evidenceA personalized plan explains daily treatment, how to recognize worsening asthma, what medicine changes were prescribed, and when to seek urgent care.
Targeted trigger reductionModerate evidenceAvoid tobacco smoke and address triggers that clearly worsen the individual’s asthma. Broad, costly environmental changes without a demonstrated trigger are less likely to help.
A quick-relief inhaler can ease symptoms but does not replace anti-inflammatory treatment when that is prescribed. Do not change an inhaler plan without the prescriber’s guidance.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: GINA 2026 Summary Guide, NHLBI asthma symptoms, NHLBI treatment and action plan, and NHLBI asthma attack guidance.
OA
Joints & movement

Osteoarthritis

A condition in which cartilage and other tissues within a joint change and break down over time, leading to pain, stiffness, swelling, or reduced function.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Osteoarthritis affects the whole joint—not only cartilage—and is not simply “wear and tear.” It commonly involves the hands, knees, hips, neck, or lower back. Diagnosis uses the symptom pattern and examination; imaging or laboratory tests may help when the diagnosis is uncertain or another cause needs to be excluded.

What people may notice

Pain often worsens with use, and stiffness after waking or resting usually lasts less than 30 minutes. Swelling, reduced motion, grinding sensations, bony enlargement, or a joint that feels unstable can occur. Symptoms and x-ray changes do not always match closely.

When to get urgent help

A suddenly hot, very swollen, red or discolored joint—especially with fever or feeling ill—is not typical of routine osteoarthritis and needs urgent assessment for infection, gout, or another cause. Seek prompt care after major injury, inability to bear weight, a locked joint, or sudden loss of function.

Useful tracking

Record the joint involved, pain and stiffness timing, swelling, walking or hand function, sleep effects, activity, instability or falls, medicines used, and response. Function trends—such as stairs, chair rises, walking distance, or grip tasks—can be more informative than pain alone.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Individualized exerciseStrong evidenceAerobic, strengthening, aquatic, balance, or range-of-motion activity can reduce symptoms and improve function. The best program is one matched to the affected joint, ability, and preferences.
Self-management and tai chiStrong evidenceStructured self-management programs and tai chi are guideline-supported options, particularly for knee or hip osteoarthritis.
Weight management when relevantStrong evidenceFor people with overweight or obesity and knee or hip osteoarthritis, weight loss can reduce joint load, pain, and disability; goals should be supportive and realistic.
Topical NSAIDs for selected jointsStrong evidenceTopical NSAIDs are strongly recommended for knee osteoarthritis and conditionally recommended for hand osteoarthritis. They are unlikely to help a deep joint such as the hip.
Treatment depends on the joint, symptom burden, mobility goals, fall risk, kidney and cardiovascular health, gastrointestinal history, other medicines, and preferences. Surgery is considered only in selected cases after individualized evaluation.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: NIAMS osteoarthritis overview, NIAMS diagnosis and treatment guide, and 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation osteoarthritis guideline.
MH
Mental health

Depression

A treatable mood disorder that can cause persistent sadness or loss of interest together with changes in thinking, sleep, energy, appetite, movement, or daily function.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Major depression is diagnosed when a defined pattern of symptoms is present most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks and causes meaningful distress or impairment. A clinician also considers grief, substance use, medicines, medical conditions, bipolar disorder, and other explanations; a screening score alone is not a diagnosis.

What people may notice

Sadness, emptiness, hopelessness, irritability, loss of interest or pleasure, guilt, low energy, slowed or restless movement, sleep or appetite changes, concentration difficulty, unexplained aches, and thoughts of death can occur. Depression may look different across ages and individuals.

When to get urgent help

Thoughts of suicide, an urge to self-harm, inability to stay safe, psychosis, or severe symptoms that stop someone from eating or drinking need immediate help. In the U.S., call or text 988; call 911 for an immediate life-threatening situation. Elsewhere, use local emergency or crisis services.

Useful tracking

Track mood, interest, sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, daily function, substance use, medicines and side effects, therapy attendance, and any change in suicidal thinking. Share worsening agitation, unusually elevated energy with little need for sleep, or abrupt behavior changes promptly.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Evidence-based psychotherapyStrong evidenceCognitive behavioral therapy is a recommended initial option for adults with major depression; other structured therapies may also be appropriate based on the person and available care.
Second-generation antidepressantsStrong evidenceThese medicines are a recommended initial option for moderate-to-severe major depression. Choice depends on symptoms, side effects, other conditions, interactions, prior response, pregnancy, cost, and preferences.
Psychotherapy plus medicationModerate evidenceCombined treatment is a reasonable initial alternative for some moderate-to-severe presentations and may be useful when one approach alone is insufficient.
Exercise as an adjunctModerate evidenceRegular activity can support mood and health when feasible, but it should not be framed as a test of willpower or a substitute for needed clinical care.
Treatment should be selected collaboratively and reassessed. Antidepressants can take time to help, may initially cause side effects, and should not be started, stopped, or changed without a prescriber’s guidance.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: NIMH depression overview, NIMH help and crisis guidance, ACP major depression guideline, and FDA fluoxetine prescribing information.
CKD
Kidney health

Chronic kidney disease

Abnormal kidney structure or function that persists for more than three months and has implications for health, often identified through blood and urine testing before symptoms appear.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) means kidney damage or reduced function has persisted long enough to be chronic. Evaluation uses estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), the underlying cause, and other findings. One abnormal result often needs confirmation rather than being interpreted alone.

What people may notice

Early CKD usually causes no symptoms. With more advanced disease, swelling, fatigue, sleep difficulty, itching, poor appetite, nausea, changes in urination, muscle cramps, or shortness of breath may occur. These symptoms are not specific to CKD.

When to get urgent help

Seek urgent care for severe shortness of breath, chest pain, confusion, fainting, seizure, inability to keep fluids down, a marked fall in urine output, or rapidly worsening swelling. Acute illness, dehydration, or a new medicine can cause sudden kidney injury on top of CKD and may require same-day advice.

Useful tracking

Keep eGFR and UACR trends, blood pressure, weight and swelling, diabetes measures when relevant, prescribed doses, and laboratory monitoring together. Include over-the-counter pain relievers, supplements, contrast procedures, illnesses, and medication changes because kidney function affects medicine safety.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Blood pressure control and indicated ACE inhibitor or ARB therapyStrong evidenceBlood pressure management is foundational. ACE inhibitors or ARBs can reduce albuminuria and protect the kidneys in appropriate groups, with creatinine and potassium monitoring.
SGLT2 inhibitors for eligible patientsStrong evidenceThese medicines reduce kidney and cardiovascular risk in guideline-defined CKD groups with or without diabetes. Eligibility depends on eGFR, albuminuria, heart failure, and individual safety factors.
Diabetes management when relevantStrong evidenceAn individualized glucose plan helps reduce complications, but medicine selection and targets must account for kidney function and hypoglycemia risk.
Individualized nutrition, activity, and tobacco supportModerate evidenceSodium, protein, potassium, and fluid advice should match CKD stage, laboratory values, nutritional status, and other conditions rather than follow a universal “kidney diet.”
CKD stage and risk are not determined by eGFR alone. Cause, albuminuria, rate of change, age, complications, cardiovascular risk, and treatment goals all matter. Avoid starting or stopping medicines based only on a single result.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: NIDDK CKD overview, NIDDK CKD testing guide, NIDDK CKD management guide, and KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline.
AD
Skin health · Allergy & immune

Atopic dermatitis (eczema)

A chronic, noncontagious inflammatory skin condition that causes intense itching, dryness, irritation, and recurring flares.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

Atopic dermatitis is the most common type of eczema. Genes, immune activity, the skin barrier, and environmental exposures all contribute. It can begin at any age, affects skin tones differently, and often alternates between flares and periods of improvement. Diagnosis is clinical because several rashes can look similar.

What people may notice

Severe itch and dry, sensitive skin are typical. Patches may look red, brown, purple, gray, swollen, scaly, cracked, thickened, or darker or lighter than nearby skin. Scratching can cause bleeding, clear-fluid weeping, crusting, sleep loss, and infection.

When to get urgent help

Promptly contact a clinician for rapidly spreading pain, warmth, pus, golden crusts, fever, or feeling ill. Clusters of painful or itchy blisters with fever or swollen glands may be eczema herpeticum and require immediate medical care, especially near an eye. Trouble breathing or swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat is an emergency.

Useful tracking

Record body location, itch and sleep disruption, flare dates, bathing and moisturizing, prescribed topical use, new skin or household products, work or hobby exposures, weather, and infection signs. Avoid automatically blaming foods; testing and elimination diets should be clinically guided.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Regular moisturizersStrong evidenceFragrance-free creams or ointments support the skin barrier, reduce dryness and severity, and can increase time between flares. Apply soon after bathing and as needed.
Topical corticosteroidsStrong evidenceThese are established first-line anti-inflammatory treatments for flares. Potency, amount, site, age, and duration must be matched carefully.
Topical calcineurin inhibitorsStrong evidenceTacrolimus ointment or pimecrolimus cream can reduce inflammation and flares without steroid-related skin thinning and may be useful at sensitive sites.
Other prescription topical or systemic therapyStrong evidenceNonsteroidal topical medicines, phototherapy, biologics, or oral targeted medicines may be appropriate depending on age, severity, location, response, access, and safety considerations.
Bathing and wet-wrap therapyModerate evidenceGentle bathing followed by moisturizer can hydrate skin; clinician-guided wet wraps can help selected flares. Do not place potent topical medicine under wraps unless specifically instructed.
“Eczema” includes several conditions. A new, severe, painful, widespread, treatment-resistant, or unusual rash deserves evaluation before escalating topical treatment.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: NIAMS atopic dermatitis overview, NIAMS diagnosis and treatment guide, AAD atopic dermatitis guidelines, and AAD symptoms and infection warning signs.
COPD
Respiratory health

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

A long-term lung condition with persistent airflow obstruction, usually involving emphysema, chronic bronchitis, or both. Spirometry is needed to confirm the diagnosis.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

COPD develops after long-term exposure to lung irritants—most often tobacco smoke, but also air pollution, workplace dusts or fumes, and sometimes genetic factors. Symptoms, exacerbation history, spirometry, oxygen levels, and other conditions all shape management.

What people may notice

Progressive shortness of breath, chronic cough, sputum, wheezing, chest tightness, reduced activity tolerance, fatigue, or repeated respiratory infections may occur. Symptoms often develop gradually and can be mistaken for aging or deconditioning.

When to get urgent help

Call emergency services for severe or rapidly worsening breathlessness, inability to speak in full sentences, blue or gray lips, confusion, fainting, or chest pain. A meaningful increase in breathlessness, cough, sputum amount, or sputum color may signal a flare and warrants prompt use of the written action plan or clinical advice.

Useful tracking

Record breathlessness and activity tolerance, cough and sputum changes, rescue-inhaler use, maintenance-inhaler adherence and technique, oxygen use if prescribed, infections, smoke exposure, vaccinations, and flare dates or hospital visits.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Smoking cessation when relevantStrong evidenceStopping smoking is the most important intervention for slowing disease progression in people who smoke; counseling plus medication improves quit success.
Long-acting inhaled bronchodilatorsStrong evidenceLAMA and LABA medicines can reduce symptoms and exacerbations. Selection and combinations depend on breathlessness, flare history, eosinophils, and other conditions.
Pulmonary rehabilitationStrong evidenceSupervised exercise, education, and self-management support improve exercise capacity and quality of life, especially for symptomatic or recently hospitalized patients.
Vaccination and written action planningStrong evidenceRecommended immunizations and a personalized plan can reduce preventable infection risk and support earlier recognition of exacerbations.
Asthma, heart disease, anemia, infection, and other conditions can cause similar symptoms. Inhaler choice and oxygen therapy require individual assessment; oxygen should not be started without documented need and a prescription.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: GOLD 2026 Report and Pocket Guide, NHLBI COPD overview, and NHLBI flare and emergency guide.
GAD
Mental health

Generalized anxiety disorder

Persistent, difficult-to-control worry about multiple areas of life, accompanied by physical or cognitive symptoms and meaningful distress or impairment.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

GAD is more than temporary stress. Diagnosis considers how long worry has persisted, how difficult it is to control, its effect on daily life, related symptoms, substance or medication effects, and physical conditions that can resemble anxiety.

What people may notice

Excessive worry, restlessness, feeling on edge, fatigue, irritability, muscle tension, poor concentration, and sleep difficulty are common. Symptoms vary, and anxiety can coexist with depression, panic, trauma-related conditions, or substance use.

When to get urgent help

Seek immediate help for thoughts of suicide or self-harm, inability to stay safe, severe agitation, hallucinations, or symptoms that may be a medical emergency such as chest pressure, fainting, or severe breathing difficulty. New intense symptoms deserve assessment rather than automatic labeling as anxiety.

Useful tracking

Record worry intensity and triggers, avoidance, sleep, caffeine and substance use, physical symptoms, functioning, therapy practice, medication changes, side effects, and standardized symptom scores when used with a clinician.

ApproachEvidence snapshotWhat that means here
Cognitive behavioral therapyStrong evidenceCBT is a well-studied psychotherapy that builds skills for responding differently to worry, avoidance, and unhelpful thinking patterns.
SSRI or SNRI medicationStrong evidenceThese antidepressants can reduce GAD symptoms but take time to work; selection, monitoring, and tapering should be individualized.
Healthy sleep, activity, and caffeine reductionModerate evidenceThese habits may support symptom improvement alongside standard care but are not substitutes for needed psychotherapy or medication.
BenzodiazepinesLimited roleThey can rapidly reduce anxiety but carry sedation, tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, and interaction risks, so use is generally brief and carefully selected.
Reviewed July 2026. Source notes: NIMH generalized anxiety disorder guide and NIMH mental health medication overview.
IBS
Digestive health

Irritable bowel syndrome

A disorder of gut–brain interaction involving repeated abdominal pain and altered bowel movements without visible damage to the digestive tract.

Explore overview
Open the complete overview

What it is

IBS is diagnosed from a characteristic pattern of abdominal pain related to bowel movements plus changes in stool frequency or form. Subtypes include constipation-predominant, diarrhea-predominant, mixed, and unclassified IBS. Targeted testing may be needed to exclude other disease.

What people may notice

Abdominal pain, bloating, diarrhea, constipation, or alternating stool patterns are typical. People may also notice urgency, mucus, or a sense of incomplete emptying. Symptoms can fluctuate over months or years.

When to get urgent help

Prompt assessment is important for rectal bleeding, black stools, unexplained weight loss, anemia, fever, persistent vomiting, severe or steadily worsening pain, a new abdominal mass, or symptoms beginning later in life. Severe dehydration or fainting requires urgent care.

Useful tracking

What it is
